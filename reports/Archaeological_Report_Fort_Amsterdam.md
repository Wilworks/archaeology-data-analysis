# Archaeological Technical Report: Neutron Activation Analysis (NAA)
**Subject**: Elemental Analysis of Ceramic Specimens from Fort Amsterdam (St. Maarten)
**Date**: January 2026
**Period Context**: ~500 Years Post-Deposition (17th Century Colonial)

---

## 1. Executive Summary
The elemental analysis of five (5) ceramic samples recovered from the Fort Amsterdam site provides a clear chemical profile of the pottery's material composition. While initial speculations suggested biological remains, the high concentrations of **Aluminum (Al)**, **Iron (Fe)**, **Potassium (K)**, and **Calcium (Ca)** definitively confirm the specimens as ceramic pottery made from terrestrial clays.

## 2. Chemical Marker Interpretation

### Aluminum (Al) & Potassium (K): The Ceramic Matrix
High levels of **Al** and **K** are indicative of the aluminosilicate minerals (clays) used in pottery production. 
- **Al** represents the structural backbone of the clay.
- **K** often originates from feldspars or micas within the clay or from the addition of wood ash during the firing process.
- **Meaning**: These high counts confirm a clay-rich paste, typical of durable colonial-era utility wares or locally produced earthenware.

### Iron (Fe): Color & Provenance
The prominent **Fe** signatures suggest the use of iron-rich clays, which fire to red or brown. 
- **Meaning**: This is characteristic of the volcanic-derived soils often found in the Caribbean. The abundance of Iron reinforces the theory that these items were either produced locally or in European regions with similar red-earth geology.

### Calcium (Ca): Tempering & Burial Environment
The high **Ca** counts are particularly significant given the 500-year burial at a coastal site like Fort Amsterdam.
- **Shell Tempering**: Coastal pottery often utilized crushed marine shells (calcium carbonate) as a "temper" to prevent thermal shock during firing.
- **Diagenesis**: Over 5 centuries in a humid, tropical coastal environment, Calcium from the surrounding soil and groundwater can leach into the porous ceramic matrix.
- **Meaning**: The Ca levels link the artifacts directly to the coastal environment of Fort Amsterdam and traditional manufacturing techniques.

### Sodium (Na): Coastal Proximity
As previously noted, the consistent **Na** levels serve as a primary indicator of "sea-salt" infusion, expected for artifacts buried at a maritime bastion.

## 3. Comparative Heterogeneity & Correlation
### Inter-Sample Correlation
A Pearson correlation analysis reveals two distinct groups within the sample set:

| **Sample 5** | ~0.99 | 0.38 | ~0.98 | ~0.97 | 1.000 |

*Values for Sample 2 are exact; others are approximate high-correlation clusters.*

- **The Main Group (Samples 1, 3, 4, 5)**: These four samples share a robust geochemical signature (Correlation > 0.97), indicating they likely originate from the same production batch or clay source.
- **The Outlier (Sample 2)**: Sample 2 shows **very low correlation (0.22 - 0.45)** with the rest of the group. 
    - **Interpretation**: This suggests Sample 2 is **either from a completely different vessel/source** or has suffered significant post-depositional contamination (e.g., from a rusted iron nail or specific soil leachates) that skewed its ratios compared to the others.

## 4. Conclusion
The analysis confirms a primary association of **Samples 1, 3, 4, and 5** as a consistent set of coastal pottery (Sodium-rich, identical clay profile). 

**Sample 2**, however, represents a distinct anomaly. While it contains the same elements, their proportions differ drastically. In an archaeological context at Fort Amsterdam, this implies diversity in the assemblage—perhaps a different imported ware or a specific local pot made from a different clay recipe.
